Carbon cycle · box models

How to modela planet

If we stopped emitting CO₂ today, how long until it is gone? The question contains a false premise. Methane has a lifetime — about twelve years. Carbon dioxide does not have one at all.

This page builds the machinery for answering that properly, one box at a time, starting from something you already understand: a bathtub.

01 — The idea

A box is just bookkeeping

A box model is the least clever idea in science, and that is its strength. You draw a box around some part of the world, count what goes in, count what comes out, and whatever is left is what is inside.

Not where inside. Not what it is doing. A box has no interior geography — everything in it is assumed perfectly mixed, one number for the whole thing. That assumption is a lie, and it is the price of admission. In exchange you get a model that runs a million years of planetary chemistry in under a minute, and whose every term you can point at and defend.

dN/dt = Fin − Fout
  • dN/dt — how fast the amount in the box is changing
  • Fin — everything arriving, per year
  • Fout — everything leaving, per year

That is the whole equation. Every box, in every box model ever built, is this sentence. The rest of this page is about what you put in Fin and Fout — and how you know.

02 — One box

The bathtub

Give the outflow one property — that it is proportional to how full the box is — and the box acquires a personality. A fuller bathtub pushes harder on the drain. Written down, the outflow becomes N/τ, where τ ("tau") is the residence time: how long a typical molecule sticks around.

dN/dt = Fin − N/τ
  • N/τ — the fuller the box, the faster it drains
  • τ — residence time, in years

Two things follow, and both matter later. The box settles at N = Fin × τ — inflow times residence time, no matter where it started. And if you dump in a slug of extra carbon, the excess decays away as a clean exponential, e−t/τ.

Hold on to the exponential. The entire argument of this page is that the atmosphere is not this box — and the way you find out is that a single exponential cannot fit what really happens.

03 — Two boxes

Air and ocean

One box cannot represent a planet, because a planet has places carbon can go. Add a second box for the ocean and connect them. The connecting flux is driven by a difference: gas moves from wherever its pressure is higher toward wherever it is lower, at a rate set by how fast the sea surface can exchange.

Fas = kas ( pCO₂air − pCO₂sea )
  • Fas — net carbon crossing the sea surface
  • pCO₂ — the "pushing pressure" of CO₂ on each side
  • kas — how fast the surface can exchange; sets a timescale of about a year

Everything now hinges on one question: when you push carbon into seawater, how much does its pCO₂ push back? Answer that and you know how much the ocean will take. Answer it wrong and you will be wrong by a factor of four, in a direction that has fooled people repeatedly.

04 — Provenance

Where the numbers come from

The fair objection to any model is that its numbers were chosen to make the answer come out right. The defence is to make the choosing visible — so here it is, in full.

ANEMONE prescribes only quantities that are measured, and then derives everything else from a single requirement: that the preindustrial world sat still. If carbon was neither accumulating nor draining away in 1750, the interior of the ocean has no freedom left. Its concentrations follow from arithmetic.

      05 — The certain part

      Chemistry is not a modelling choice

      Models get argued about because they contain judgement. This part contains none. When CO₂ dissolves in seawater it reacts, and the proportions it settles into are fixed by thermodynamics — the same law that sets which way heat flows. Those proportions have been measured in laboratories to four decimal places, published with uncertainties, and re-measured by people hoping to find the first group wrong.

      Everything in the panel below is computed live from those published constants — Weiss, Lueker, Dickson, Millero, Mucci — the same code the model runs. Nothing here is tuned. Nothing here can be tuned.

      The number to watch is the Revelle factor. It answers the question from the last section: raise dissolved carbon by 1%, and pCO₂ rises by about 10%. The ocean pushes back ten times harder than you pushed. That is the buffer, and it is the single most important number in the carbon cycle.

      06 — Stage one

      The ocean as soda water

      Start with the simplest defensible ocean: CO₂ dissolves in it exactly as it dissolves in a fizzy drink, following Henry's law, and nothing further happens. Instant mixing, no chemistry, no biology.

      Two intuitions compete here, and it is worth committing to one before reading on. The reservoir argument: the ocean holds about sixty times the carbon the atmosphere does, so it should swallow around 98% of anything we emit. The soda-water argument: run Henry's law and see.

      76% still in the air, forever

      Both intuitions are wrong, and they are wrong from opposite sides. As plain soda water the ocean holds only about 200 GtC of dissolved CO₂ — less than the atmosphere. The sixty-fold reservoir is real, but almost none of it is dissolved gas. It is something else, and that something else is the next section.

      07 — Stage two

      The reaction that does the work

      Dissolved CO₂ does not stay as CO₂. It reacts with water and with the carbonate ion already in seawater, and almost all of it ends up as bicarbonate. That is where the ocean's enormous carbon inventory actually lives — not as gas, but as ions.

      CO₂ + H₂O + CO₃²⁻ ⇌ 2 HCO₃⁻
      • Incoming CO₂ is consumed, so it stops pushing back
      • But it eats a carbonate ion to do it
      • Fewer carbonate ions left ⇒ the next molecule is harder to absorb

      Adding this one reaction moves the answer by a factor of four — from 76% airborne to 18%. It is the largest single correction anywhere in the model, and it is pure thermodynamics: nothing was fitted to get it.

      08 — Stage three

      The ocean is not one box

      Stage 2 assumed the whole ocean mixes instantly. It does not. Surface water exchanges with the deep on timescales of centuries, and until it does, the deep ocean's enormous capacity is simply unavailable.

      So the ocean gets split — surface, thermocline, deep — with carbon moved between them at prescribed rates. Why three and not two? Because the real decay has both a decades-long mode and a centuries-long one, and a two-box ocean has exactly one mixing timescale. Tune it to match the century and it overshoots the millennium. This was tried first. It does not work.

      Note what this does to the answer at 100 years: it goes up, from 18% to 44%. Slowing the ocean down keeps more carbon in the air on human timescales, even though the thousand-year answer barely moves.

      09 — Stage four

      The seafloor answers

      Acidify the deep ocean and it starts to dissolve its own floor. The seabed holds a reactive layer of calcium carbonate — shells, mostly — and when the water above becomes corrosive enough, that carbonate goes into solution and hands back the alkalinity the incoming CO₂ consumed.

      This is the ocean partially repairing its own buffer. It takes thousands of years, and it pulls the airborne fraction down to about 8% at a hundred thousand years — but it cannot finish the job, because it is only redistributing carbonate that was already in the ocean system.

      Fdiss = ksed · Msed · (1 − Ω)n
      • Ω — how saturated the deep water is; below 1 it dissolves carbonate
      • Msed — the reactive carbonate available on the seafloor
      • The further below saturation, the faster it dissolves

      10 — Stage five

      The thermostat

      The last process is the slowest and the strangest: rock. CO₂ dissolved in rainwater attacks silicate minerals on land, and the products wash to the sea where they are eventually buried as carbonate. Carbon leaves the ocean–atmosphere system entirely and becomes rock again.

      What makes it a thermostat is that the reaction runs faster when the planet is warmer and when there is more CO₂ to dissolve. Push CO₂ up and weathering speeds up, drawing it back down. Let CO₂ fall and weathering slows, letting volcanoes refill the air.

      Fsil = Fsil,0 · (pCO₂/pCO₂0)0.3 · eΔT/12
      • More CO₂ in the air ⇒ faster weathering
      • Warmer ⇒ faster weathering (about an e-folding per 12 K)
      • Both terms push the planet back toward where volcanoes and weathering balance

      Left alone, the model settles at 279.99 ppm — not because anyone set it there, but because that is the CO₂ level where silicate weathering exactly consumes what volcanoes emit. The thermostat finds its own setpoint.

      11 — The licence

      Why we are allowed to assume that

      Walker, Hays & Kasting

      A negative feedback mechanism for the long-term stabilization of Earth's surface temperature

      Journal of Geophysical Research 86, 9776–9782 · 1981

      Every term in the previous section is an assumption, and assumptions in a model need a source. This is the source — known universally as WHAK, after its authors.

      The problem it solved was the faint young Sun. The Sun was about 30% dimmer early in Earth's history, which should have left the planet frozen solid. It was not: there is liquid water in the rock record throughout. Something held the temperature up while the Sun brightened, and kept holding it as conditions changed.

      WHAK's answer was that the silicate weathering feedback does it automatically. No biology required, no coincidence required. The dependence of weathering on CO₂ and temperature — the exponents in the equation above — is what makes Earth's climate self-correcting on million-year timescales.

      This is what "standing on the shoulders" actually looks like in practice. Stage 5 is four lines of code. Those four lines are licensed by forty years of work testing whether the feedback is real, and the honest caveat is that its exponents remain poorly constrained — which is why section 14 shows a band rather than a line.

      Five stages, five answers

      Here is every stage on one plot: the fraction of a 1,000 GtC pulse still in the atmosphere, against time, from one year to a million. Time is on a logarithmic axis because that is the only way five distinct timescales fit on one page.

      Toggle the stages on. Each one adds a single process, and each one changes the answer somewhere different along the axis.

      There is no single number here. The decay is a sum of exponentials spanning five orders of magnitude in time, so "the lifetime of CO₂" is not a fact about the world — it is a choice about which timescale you care about. The IPCC's own impulse response function contains a constant term with no time constant attached to it at all: 21.7% of a pulse that simply never decays. This model is what that 21.7% turns out to be.

      13 — The twist

      The ocean gets worse at its job

      One last thing, and it is the one people find hardest to believe. Everything so far described fractions — as if the ocean took a fixed share. It does not. Emit twice as much and the ocean does not take twice as much; it takes a smaller share of a bigger number.

      14 — Honesty

      What this model gets wrong

      A model you cannot criticise is not a model, it is a claim. These are the places where ANEMONE is known to be wrong, in roughly the order they would change an answer you care about.

      Ventilation is imposed, not derived

      The rates at which water moves between ocean layers were tuned to match the observed impulse response. A box model cannot derive them from physics — there is no fluid dynamics in it.

      Two mixing timescales, not a spectrum

      The real ocean ventilates across a continuum of timescales. Three boxes give two. This is most of the residual mismatch at a century.

      No land biosphere, no permafrost

      Both matter for the first century specifically — exactly the period most people are asking about.

      Warming is instantaneous

      The model applies equilibrium climate sensitivity with no ocean thermal inertia. Fine for the weathering feedback; wrong if you want the zero-emissions commitment.

      Weathering exponents are loosely constrained

      Sweeping the silicate exponent across its plausible range visibly moves the 400,000-year tail. The honest presentation is a band, not a line.

      Radiocarbon says the deep ocean is too young

      Tuned to the century-scale airborne fraction, the model's deep water comes out about 40‰ too young in radiocarbon. Correct for the timescale it was tuned for, too vigorous for the millennial deep ocean.

      One thing this result does not mean. CO₂ persisting for millennia does not imply warming keeps increasing for millennia. If emissions stopped, temperature would stay roughly flat rather than continue climbing, because falling CO₂ approximately cancels the lag from ocean thermal inertia. Long CO₂ lifetime and continued warming are different claims, and conflating them is the most common error made about this science.
